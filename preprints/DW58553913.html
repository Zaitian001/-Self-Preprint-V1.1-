1. Introduction

1.1 From Early Warning to Forecasting

Earthquakes remain among the most difficult natural hazards to anticipate because rupture nucleation emerges from a complex, heterogeneous, nonlinear fault system whose state cannot be completely observed.

A conventional earthquake early-warning system does not predict earthquake initiation. Instead, it detects an earthquake after rupture begins and attempts to estimate the event and warn locations that have not yet experienced the strongest shaking. This approach can provide seconds to tens of seconds of warning under favorable conditions and remains an important component of earthquake-risk reduction.

The scientific problem addressed in this paper is different. The central question is:

$$ \boxed{ \textit{Can measurable changes in the physical state of the Earth be detected before macroscopic rupture,} } $$

and, if so,

$$ \boxed{ \textit{can independent precursor channels be combined to progressively narrow the probability of impending seismic activity?} } $$

This distinction is essential. The present work therefore uses forecasting rather than deterministic prediction as its primary scientific objective.

A deterministic earthquake prediction would require specification of location, time, and magnitude. No reliable general method currently provides all three for an individual future earthquake. A probabilistic forecasting system has a different objective:

$$ P(E \mid \mathcal D_t), $$

where $E$ represents a future seismic event and $\mathcal D_t$ represents all observations available up to time $t$. The goal is not to claim certainty. The goal is to determine whether

$$ P(E\mid\mathcal D_t) > P_0(E) $$

with sufficient statistical significance, reliability, calibration, and lead time to support practical risk reduction.

1.2 The Trans-Scale Problem

Earthquake rupture is a multiscale process. At the smallest scales, microscopic defects, grain boundaries, fluid-assisted cracking, and nanoscale fracture processes contribute to material failure. At larger scales, microcracking and acoustic emissions emerge. At still larger scales, deformation, slow-slip, fluid migration, electromagnetic effects, and seismicity become observable. Finally, macroscopic rupture generates elastic waves that propagate through the Earth.

The observational challenge is therefore not simply

$$ \text{high frequency}\rightarrow\text{low frequency}. $$

It is a more general transformation:

$$ \boxed{ \text{microscopic state} \rightarrow \text{mesoscopic organization} \rightarrow \text{fault-scale preparation} \rightarrow \text{macroscopic rupture}. } $$

The central hypothesis of this work is that if such a cascade contains measurable information, the information may be distributed across multiple observational channels rather than concentrated in a single precursor. This motivates a trans-scale state-space formulation.

1.3 The Candidate Nucleation Layer

Recent experimental studies have investigated emissions generated during fracture of rocks, including acoustic, electromagnetic, and neutron signals. More recent work has proposed that nanoscale mechanical instabilities can generate terahertz-frequency phonons and that these processes may be associated with particle emissions.

These observations are scientifically interesting because they suggest that fracture may generate measurable signatures well below the frequency range of conventional seismology. However, a critical distinction must be maintained:

$$ \boxed{ \text{fracture emission} \neq \text{validated earthquake precursor}. } $$

A laboratory fracture signal does not automatically establish that the same signal can be detected remotely in the Earth's crust or that it systematically precedes large earthquakes. Accordingly, this paper treats THz and particle emissions as a candidate nucleation channel.

The relevant hypothesis is:

$$ H_{\rm N}: \quad \text{statistically significant anomalies in nucleation-scale emissions} $$

may contain information about the evolving state of a fault system. The competing null hypothesis is:

$$ H_0: \quad \text{observed anomalies are explainable by background variability, instrumental effects, environmental forcing, or unrelated processes}. $$

Only prospective testing can distinguish these hypotheses.

3.1 The Stress-Driven Metric Evolution Law

We consider a fault system as a complex adaptive system whose states are confined to a differentiable seismic state manifold $\mathcal{M}$ of dimension $d = \dim \mathcal{M} > 2$. The local geometric structures of this parameter space—such as structural distinguishability, sensitivity, and transport efficiency—are determined by a data-derived, smooth Riemannian metric tensor $g_{\mu\nu}$. In a baseline locked state, $g_{\mu\nu}$ maintains a configuration dominated by negative scalar curvature $R < 0$, establishing a convergent topological landscape where natural background fluctuations are structurally guided back into stable locked basins.

To model the progression of seismic nucleation, we introduce a localized, non-stationary tectonic stress field $\chi(x, t) \in \mathbb{R}^k$, representing sustained tectonic loading, pore pressure diffusion, or thermal stress. We hypothesize that prolonged exposure to $\chi$ forces a dynamic, non-equilibrium deformation of the underlying manifold geometry. We formalize this process via a covariant geometric flow equation governing the rate of metric variation:

$$ \frac{\partial g_{\mu\nu}}{\partial t} = -2 \gamma_{\rm response} R_{\mu\nu} + \alpha_{\rm stress} T_{\mu\nu}(\chi) - \beta_{\rm dissipation} g_{\mu\nu} \quad (7) $$

where:

  • $R_{\mu\nu}$ is the Ricci curvature tensor of $(\mathcal{M}, g)$;
  • $\gamma_{\rm response} > 0$ represents the intrinsic relaxation rate of the fault attempting to return to locked configuration;
  • $\beta_{\rm dissipation} > 0$ denotes the spontaneous healing or stress dissipation rate;
  • $\alpha_{\rm stress}$ defines the structural coupling susceptibility of the seismic state space to the stress energy-momentum tensor $T_{\mu\nu}(\chi)$.

To ensure strict coordinate invariance, $T_{\mu\nu}(\chi)$ is constructed from the first covariant derivatives of the stress field:

$$ T_{\mu\nu}(\chi) = \nabla_\mu \chi \nabla_\nu \chi - \frac{1}{2} g_{\mu\nu} g^{\alpha\beta} \nabla_\alpha \chi \nabla_\beta \chi. \quad (8) $$

Here $\nabla$ denotes the Levi-Civita connection associated with $g_{\mu\nu}$.

3.2 Infinitesimal Variations of the Connection and Ricci Tensors

To map how the stress tensor deforms local state trajectories, we evaluate the variations of the geometric invariants over an incremental time step $\delta t$. Let the instantaneous time-derivative of the metric tensor be denoted by the symmetric tensor field $h_{\mu\nu}$:

$$ h_{\mu\nu} \equiv \frac{\partial g_{\mu\nu}}{\partial t}. \quad (9) $$

By taking the derivative of the identity $g_{\mu\alpha} g^{\alpha\nu} = \delta_\mu^\nu$, the corresponding variation of the inverse metric is

$$ \frac{\partial g^{\mu\nu}}{\partial t} = -g^{\mu\alpha} g^{\nu\beta} h_{\alpha\beta} \equiv -h^{\mu\nu}. \quad (10) $$

The variation of the Christoffel symbols $\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\nu}$ under $h_{\mu\nu}$ does not depend on coordinate choices and transforms as a genuine tensor field. Applying Palatini's identity, the time evolution of the connection components resolves to:

$$ \frac{\partial \Gamma^\rho_{\mu\nu}}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{2} g^{\rho\sigma} \left( \nabla_\mu h_{\nu\sigma} + \nabla_\nu h_{\mu\sigma} - \nabla_\sigma h_{\mu\nu} \right). \quad (11) $$

The Riemann curvature tensor $R^\rho_{\;\sigma\mu\nu}$ is defined via the commutator of covariant derivatives. Taking the time derivative of the Riemann components and contracting over indices yields the generalized Palatini variation for the Ricci tensor:

$$ \frac{\partial R_{\mu\nu}}{\partial t} = \nabla_\rho \left( \frac{\partial \Gamma^\rho_{\mu\nu}}{\partial t} \right) - \nabla_\nu \left( \frac{\partial \Gamma^\rho_{\mu\rho}}{\partial t} \right). \quad (12) $$

Substituting Eq. (11) into Eq. (12) and commuting covariant derivatives yields:

$$ \frac{\partial R_{\mu\nu}}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \nabla^\rho \nabla_\mu h_{\nu\rho} + \nabla^\rho \nabla_\nu h_{\mu\rho} - \Delta_g h_{\mu\nu} - \nabla_\mu \nabla_\nu h \right), \quad (13) $$

where $\Delta_g \equiv \nabla^\rho \nabla_\rho$ is the Beltrami–Laplace operator on $(\mathcal{M}, g)$, and $h \equiv h^\alpha_{\;\alpha} = g^{\alpha\beta} h_{\alpha\beta}$ is the trace of the metric deformation tensor.

3.3 Derivation of the Scalar Curvature Propagation Equation

The scalar curvature $R$ is the trace of the Ricci tensor:

$$ R = g^{\mu\nu} R_{\mu\nu}. $$

Differentiating with respect to time gives two geometric contributions:

$$ \frac{\partial R}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial g^{\mu\nu}}{\partial t} R_{\mu\nu} + g^{\mu\nu} \frac{\partial R_{\mu\nu}}{\partial t}. \quad (14) $$

Substituting Eq. (10) and Eq. (13) into Eq. (14):

$$ \frac{\partial R}{\partial t} = -h^{\mu\nu} R_{\mu\nu} + \frac{1}{2} g^{\mu\nu} \left( \nabla^\rho \nabla_\mu h_{\nu\rho} + \nabla^\rho \nabla_\nu h_{\mu\rho} - \Delta_g h_{\mu\nu} - \nabla_\mu \nabla_\nu h \right). \quad (15) $$

Distributing $g^{\mu\nu}$ and using metric compatibility $\nabla_\rho g_{\mu\nu} = 0$, we collect and simplify the covariant derivative terms:

$$ g^{\mu\nu} \nabla^\rho \nabla_\mu h_{\nu\rho} = \nabla^\rho \nabla^\nu h_{\nu\rho} = \nabla^\mu \nabla^\nu h_{\mu\nu}, \quad (16) $$
$$ g^{\mu\nu} \Delta_g h_{\mu\nu} = \Delta_g (g^{\mu\nu} h_{\mu\nu}) = \Delta_g h, \quad (17) $$
$$ g^{\mu\nu} \nabla_\mu \nabla_\nu h = \Delta_g h. \quad (18) $$

Combining these contractions transforms Eq. (15) into the general, coordinate-invariant geometric equation for scalar curvature propagation:

$$ \frac{\partial R}{\partial t} = -\Delta_g h + \nabla^\mu \nabla^\nu h_{\mu\nu} - h^{\mu\nu} R_{\mu\nu}. \quad (19) $$

Equation (19) is the exact propagation identity used below. It is the starting point for the seismic specialization.

3.4 The Curvature Inversion Equation and Pathological Phase Transitions

To isolate a candidate mechanism of pathological landscape restructuring, we specialize Eq. (19) to a regime where stress-tensor gradients dominate short-term structural variations. In this regime, the metric deformation tensor is approximated by the stress contribution:

$$ h_{\mu\nu} \approx \alpha_{\rm stress} T_{\mu\nu}(\chi). \quad (20) $$

Contracting Eq. (8) with the inverse metric gives the scalar trace of the stress perturbation field:

$$ h = g^{\mu\nu} h_{\mu\nu} = \alpha_{\rm stress} \left( \frac{2 - d}{2} \right) \|\nabla \chi\|^2, \quad (21) $$

where $\|\nabla \chi\|^2 \equiv g^{\alpha\beta} \nabla_\alpha \chi \nabla_\beta \chi$.

We now substitute Eq. (20) and Eq. (21) into Eq. (19). The three terms on the right-hand side of Eq. (19) are handled as follows.

Term 1 — $-\Delta_g h$:

$$ -\Delta_g h = -\alpha_{\rm stress} \left( \frac{2 - d}{2} \right) \Delta_g \|\nabla \chi\|^2 = \alpha_{\rm stress} \left( \frac{d - 2}{2} \right) \Delta_g \|\nabla \chi\|^2. \quad (22) $$

$$ \nabla^\mu \nabla^\nu h_{\mu\nu} = \alpha_{\rm stress} \nabla^\mu \nabla^\nu \left( \nabla_\mu \chi \nabla_\nu \chi - \frac{1}{2} g_{\mu\nu} \|\nabla \chi\|^2 \right). \quad (23) $$

$$ -h^{\mu\nu} R_{\mu\nu} = -\alpha_{\rm stress} T^{\mu\nu}(\chi) R_{\mu\nu}. \quad (24) $$

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\partial R}{\partial t} = \alpha_{\rm stress} \left[ \left( \frac{d - 2}{2} \right) \Delta_g \|\nabla \chi\|^2 + \nabla^\mu \nabla^\nu \left( \nabla_\mu \chi \nabla_\nu \chi - \frac{1}{2} g_{\mu\nu} \|\nabla \chi\|^2 \right) \right] - \alpha_{\rm stress} T^{\mu\nu}(\chi) R_{\mu\nu} - \Delta_g R - 2 \gamma_{\rm response} \|{\rm Ric}\|^2 + \beta_{\rm dissipation} R. } \quad (25) $$

where $\|{\rm Ric}\|^2 \equiv R_{\mu\nu} R^{\mu\nu}$ represents the squared norm of the Ricci tensor.

(25) uses three simplifications. First, the term $-\Delta_g h$ is rewritten using the trace identity Eq. (21). Second, the term $\nabla^\mu \nabla^\nu h_{\mu\nu}$ is retained in full covariant form. Third, the term $-h^{\mu\nu} R_{\mu\nu}$ is retained explicitly, while the Ricci-flow contribution $-2\gamma_{\rm response} \|{\rm Ric}\|^2$ and the dissipation term $\beta_{\rm dissipation} R$ are inherited from the full flow Eq. (7). The term $-\Delta_g R$ is a phenomenological Laplacian damping term for the scalar curvature. Full derivation and justification are given in DW58553909. In this paper, Eq. Appendix B. Terminology

Earthquake Early Warning (EEW): notification issued after earthquake initiation but before strong shaking reaches a target location.

Phase-locking value: a measure of phase synchronization between two signals.

Sonification: transformation of latent physical-state variables into an auditory representation.

Representational anchor: a mathematical structure used to organize or encode observations; not necessarily a physical constant.
